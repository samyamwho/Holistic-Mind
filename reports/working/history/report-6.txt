2 Review of Existing Knowledge
Personalised recommendation systems are being increasingly proposed as a solution
to improve relevance, engagement, and clinical outcome in the digital world of mental
health platforms. One of the more recent definitions of these systems is that they
can provide individuals with custom materials for coping, such as articles to read,
exercises to complete, or therapy sessions to attend, based on an individual’s history
and actions within the application (Al-Sarhan et al., 2023).
Methods like content-based filtering are often employed within these systems, though
collaborative filtering approaches and knowledge-driven logic are also used depending
on context. Hybrid models appear too, especially where flexibility matters most.
Each method adjusts carefully to the unique demands of mental health information
(Al-Muqrin et al., 2024).
Matthews et al.’s framework provides a useful lens through which to understand per-
sonalisation in mental health tools, categorising approaches as rule-driven, evolving,
or guided by people. By providing individuals with suggestions based upon clearly
outlined reasoning within these applications, systems can tailor the type of coping
mechanism or goal-based activities presented to each individual according to their
personal aims (Matthews et al., 2025). At the same time, these works caution that
personalisation must be grounded in clinical models, not only in usage data, to avoid
exposing users to potentially harmful or irrelevant content.
Existing Recommendation-Based Mental Health Platforms
Emerging uses of recommender systems in mental health show early signs of boosting
how people interact with tailored support. Systems like Ginger1 ask individuals
to input information regarding their goals, mood, and interactions in order to
provide customised recommendations over time (Al-Sarhan et al., 2023). In another
case, NarraGive2, part of the NEON ecosystem, recommends stories for individuals
based upon similarities between named items and individual behaviours within the

User Modelling and Context-Aware Adaptation
An essential enabling factor for personalised recommendation and self-regulation
support is user modelling from multiple digital data sources. The recent study on
digital mental health provides examples of how user models can be derived from
self-report information, mood monitoring records, and passive sensing data including
physical activity, sleep quality, and location to estimate current affective states and
behaviour trends (Larsen et al., 2020; Gosetto et al., 2023). These models enable
adaptive feedback mechanisms, timely reminders, and interventions consistent with
the concept of the context-aware recommender system for mental health (Larsen
et al., 2020; Gosetto et al., 2023).
For instance, Gosetto et al. developed a protocol for the customisation of mobile
applications to encourage users to exhibit healthy behaviours through user modelling
principles (Gosetto et al., 2023). Although developed with general health behaviours
in mind, these techniques could be applied to mental health through the correlation
between individuals’ mobile application profiles and methods for self-regulating
nervous system states. Furthermore, frameworks for mobile applications that adapt
to users according to contextual data suggest that mHealth applications could utilise
sensor data to provide self-regulation techniques at the appropriate moment to
mitigate symptoms of stress or low mood (Barreto et al., 2023).
Ethical, Technical, and Design Challenges
Despite promising advances in the field, several challenges persist in integrating
recommendation systems into mental health applications. Among these are data
privacy, security, and safety concerns, particularly regarding the sensitive personal
health data that these systems collect (Al-Muqrin et al., 2024). There is also a need

2.1 Critical Analysis and Gap Identification
In the present context, there are alot of research reagrading recomendation system
in mental heal yet there are several missing pieces. Experts claim that the majority
of these academic writings explored the basic structure of recommendation systems
as a simple idea. These recommendation systems were only studied through certain
digital systems because generalisable design principles were not developed.(Al-Sarhan
et al., 2023; Matthews et al., 2025; Mukhiya et al., 2025). Second, there is limited
work that explicitly integrates self-regulation theory including emotion regulation
frameworks, coping strategy selection, and nervous system state modelling into the
design of recommendation algorithms for mental health applications. Most existing
systems route users to content based on general preferences or historical engagement,
rather than moment-to-moment physiological and affective state.
Third, and most critically for this project, no existing application has been designed
specifically around the intersection of childhood trauma and ADHD, despite the high
co-occurrence of these conditions and the distinct interaction patterns and content
needs they produce. (Al-Muqrin et al., 2024; Almuqrin et al., 2025).
2.2 Project Justification
This project addresses the identified gaps by designing, building, and evaluating a
trauma-informed mobile application that integrates user modelling, context-aware
adaptation, and explicit recommendation logic for coping tools and psychoeducation
content. Unlike existing platforms, The Holistic Mind places nervous system state
captured through a daily five-option check-in at the centre of its recommendation
engine. The engine combines rule-based logic (mapping check-in state to exercise
type) with preference learning (updating weights based on user ratings) to produce
personalised tool recommendations that are both clinically grounded and responsive
to moment-to-moment need.

support and unmet needs. Results are compared against a non-personalised baseline
condition in which participants receive random tool suggestions.

4.2 Justification of Methods
Design Science Research was selected over purely empirical approaches (e.g., ran-
domised controlled trials) because the primary contribution of this project is an
artefact (the application) rather than a measurement of population-level treatment
efficacy. DSR explicitly legitimises artefact construction as a research activity, pro-
vided the artefact is evaluated rigorously (Hevner et al., 2004). This is consistent
with the FYP scope and with similar work in the digital mental health literature
(Gosetto et al., 2023; Barreto et al., 2023).
A rule-based recommendation engine with preference learning was chosen over more
complex machine learning approaches (e.g., collaborative filtering, deep reinforcement
learning) for two reasons. First, the user base at evaluation stage is small, making
data-hungry approaches inappropriate. Second, rule-based logic is interpretable like
a critical requirement in mental health contexts where recommendation transparency
is ethically important (Al-Muqrin et al., 2024; Matthews et al., 2025).
Mixed methods evaluation was preferred over purely quantitative measurement
because the target population (trauma and ADHD) may find lengthy question-
naires cognitively taxing, and because qualitative data captures the nuance of lived
experience that Likert-scale instruments cannot.

5.3 Ethical Considerations
This research is going to include a particularly vulnerable group, that is why it
requires ethical design and management.
Vulnerable population. The users in the current application consists of adults
with past traumatic experiences from their childhood. No diagnosis statements will
be included within the application so that to ensure transparent ways of leaving the
platform and promote it as a self-regulatory application rather than a replacement
for therapy.
Informed consent. All participants give their written informed consent before
taking part in the experiment. It contains the description of the study, including its
purpose and procedure, as well as information about how data will be used and the
opportunity to leave the study at any point.
Data privacy and confidentiality. Participant’s data will be safely stored on
Firebase. The app doesn’t analyse journal notes and doesn’t use them for modelling.
All participants will remain anonymous during the entire experiment. All participant
data will be permanently deleted according to GDPR guidelines.
