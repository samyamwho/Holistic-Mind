Abstract
In 2021, the elderly population in Nepal accounted for 10.21% of the country’s total population,
and the increasing youth migration resulted in millions of elderly people without family support.
None of the existing commercial voice assistants (Alexa, Siri, Google Assistant) offer a functional
Nepali pipeline, none of them work without an internet connection and none of the following age-
specific features are available that are useful to an elderly Nepali user: medication reminders,
emergency auto-dial, fall detection, support for Bikram Sambat calendar. No Nepali voice
assistant has been tested with senior citizens before.
This report brings you Saathi (साथी, “companion”), a completely offline and privacy preserving
Nepali voice. assistant for elderly users, developed and evaluated through iterative prototyping
(32 weeks). The system is completely local Wi-Fi network based, with no voice or personal data
sent outside the network, and is delivered as an Android application with a WCAG AAA contrast
level, one-button interface.
After systematic iteration came three architectural results. First, Nepali ASR performance
bounded by architecture, not data: Three Whisper fine tuning configurations all reached 48.56%
Word Error Rate (WER) on the held out set, while switching to an NVIDIA NeMo Conformer-
RNNT with two-stage fine tuning, resulted in a WER of 10.10% on the held out set and 30.50% on
the public FLEURS ne_np benchmark. Second, a hybrid approach is required to achieve reliable
natural language generation for a low resource language: all nine standalone configurations of
generative models failed, whereas, the combination of deterministic Python handlers, the LLM
persona prompting (Qwen3 9B), and bilingual retrieval and generation (BAR) was successful
where none worked individually. Third, for elderly speech, in which there was a 25.1% WER
(15% higher than the baseline for adults), the functional outcomes were near-perfect in structural
fallback.
The final system achieves 99.79% validation accuracy on 22 intents using IndicBERT-v3-1B
(LoRA fine-tuning), with 100% reliability, provides medication reminders in 40 controlled tests
under Android Doze Mode, and is able to identify falls with 90% accuracy and with a single
false-positive in 10 fall tests. Formal evaluation was conducted with 7 elderly participants (aged
60 years or older) from Nepal, who answered to 147 live spoken queries across 21 intent
scenarios, with an overall Task Success Rate of 98.6% (95% CI: 95.6%–99.5%), and all queries
took less than the 15 second latency goal in a fully offline network.

1 Introduction and Context
There is a constant development of digital and assistive technologies all over the world. Google
Assistant, Siri, and Alexa have shown their usefulness in assisting people to live independently;
manage reminders, make calls and access information, just by voice (Hoy, 2018). Voice
interaction is especially well suited for older users, who are less able to use touchscreens
because of diminished vision, shaky hands, and cognitive challenges with complex touchscreen
interfaces (Stigall et al., 2019, Pradhan et al., 2020). Most commercial voice assistants, however,
are voice assistants for English language and other well-resourced languages, and are developed
for general population and not by elderly users with specific daily living needs (Shahi and Sitaula,
2021). This is not a minor difficulty for an older Nepalese speaker, rather it is a real challenge to
live independently.
The older Nepalese user’s needs include the basic things to remind him/her about medicine,
to dial emergency numbers, to provide information on the Bikram Sambat calendar, today’s
Tithi, and the upcoming festivals, simple health related advice instructions, allowing music to be
played by voice command, and automatic fall detection and emergency notification. Currently,
there is no system that offers any of these in Nepali.
There are a number of interrelated problems to be addressed in building such a system. The
Nepali language is a low resource language with not many labeled speech resources (Ghimire
et al., 2024). The Devanagari script results in word level transcription errors that change word
meaning (Shahi and Sitaula, 2021). Elderly speakers exhibit acoustical properties (slower
speech, tremor, lower voice level) that make ASR systems, trained on younger adult speakers,
perform poorly (Vipperla et al., 2010). Response generation needs to be warm, natural and
factually correct, while not hallucinating on deterministic queries. For privacy and reliability in
Nepal, an entire system must be operational without any Internet connection.
Saathi (साथी, which means companion), the design project discussed, tackles all of these chal-
lenges in an integrated system that was built and tested over 32 weeks of iterative prototyping.
Key contributions of the design, implementation, and evaluation results are provided in the
following sections.

1.1 Problem Statement
With a growing population of elderly people (Adhikari, 2023) and high youth migration rates,
commercial voice assistants fail to provide the same support services for old people (Lamich-
hane, 2024) due to language barriers (Shahi and Sitaula, 2021, Joshi et al., 2020) and lack of
age-specific features like medication reminders, emergency auto dial, and culturally relevant
information (Stigall et al., 2019, Pradhan et al., 2020), preventing this underserved population
to access assistive AI technology that has become commonplace in high resource language
communities (Hoy, 2018).
2 Review of Existing Knowledge
A literature review of previous academic research was carried out in eight thematic areas. The
analysis outlines the current extent of knowledge which directly drives this project.
2.1 Ageing Population and Need of Assistive Technology
According to the World Health Organization (World Health Organization, 2025), the number of
elderly people is expected to double by 2050. In Nepal, the elderly population grew from 6.50%
to 10.21% between 2001 and 2021 (Adhikari, 2023). As the age advances, there is a decrease
in physical mobility, cognition and memory which has a significant impact on activities of daily
living (ADL) (Czaja et al., 2006). The deployment of assistive technologies is one of the key
answers to these challenges and it has been proved that assistive technologies can facilitate
elderly people’s independence and wellbeing (Antunes et al., 2019, Marasinghe, 2015). The
demand for automated assistive systems in Nepal is even more pressing and escalating due
to high youth migration rates affecting family dynamics and leaving elderly parents with lesser
family support (Lamichhane, 2024).
2.2 Digital Health and mHealth in Low- and Middle-Income Countries
With the increasing availability of mobile devices, the use of mobile health (mHealth) services,
which are delivered through mobile devices, have been rapidly increasing in Low- and Middle-
Income Countries (LMICs). Källander et al. (2013) examined 23 mHealth studies in LMICs
and reported voice-based and SMS-based apps yielded significantly higher adoption rates
among older and less literate audiences due to avoiding the need to read and the cognitive load
2
--- PAGE 18 ---
such reading places. In LMICs, Beratarrechea et al. (2014) determined that automated voice
reminders for medication adherence had up to a 20% improvement in adherence over standard
care.
The digital health landscape in Nepal is growing rapidly: Nepal has witnessed more than 82%
of the Nepali smartphone market dominated by Android (StatCounter Global Stats, 2024), and
the government has introduced their Digital Nepal Framework to increase the use of digital
services in the rural and elderly communities. Recent findings by Acharya et al. (2023) reveal
that digital health tools in Nepal are mostly in English or in need of a high level of literacy, which
systematically excludes elderly users. This structural exclusion directly influences this project –
a voice-first, Nepali language system that is offline means that it doesn’t need to be read and is
installed on standard Android devices.
2.3 Voice Assistants for Elderly Users
Voice User Interfaces (VUIs) have a significant benefit over the traditional Graphical User
Interfaces (GUIs) when we consider elderly users (Song et al., 2022, Stigall et al., 2019). Due to
cognitive decline, shaking hands and poor vision, elderly people struggle with using touchscreens
and complex applications (Pradhan et al., 2020). Voice assistants are shown to be able to be
adapted to by elderly users, and their adoption is promising for supporting the independent living
for elderly users (Jakob et al., 2025, Chen et al., 2023).
Research, however, has also been consistently identifying failure modes that are directly relevant
to this project. The study of Trajkova and Martin-Hammond (2020) revealed that voice assistants
often fail to capture the elderly user’s speech and are therefore frequently abandoned after the
initial frustrations. Knowing what commands the system understands, called discoverability,
has been a recurring challenge identified by Pradhan et al. (2020). There is one major usability
issue that occurs with error recovery: when a system misinterprets, users do not know how
to fix it (Abdolrahmani et al., 2018). All 3 studies have been undertaken with English users of
English versions. If the language is not in use, the problem is very much greater. Motivating the
cultural localisation approach taken in this project. Pal et al. (2019) found that culturally based
interactions boost trust and adoption of digital tools for resource-constrained users in a Global
South setting.
3
--- PAGE 19 ---
2.4 Speech Technologies for Low-Resource Languages
Nepali is considered to be a low resource language due to lack of labelled speech data and
resources (Shahi and Sitaula, 2021, Jha, 2024). Previous research on Nepali ASR employed
HMM/GMM systems (Sarma and Sarma, 2010, Paudel et al., 2023) that had moderate perfor-
mance on the isolated-word tasks, were brittle, involved hand-crafted phonetic dictionaries and
were unable to process conversational speech.
Things changed drastically when self-supervised and multilingual end-to-end models appeared
in the field. The speech recognition systems like Wav2Vec 2.0 (Baevski et al., 2020), XLS-
R (Babu et al., 2021) and OpenAI Whisper (Radford et al., 2023) are able to achieve good
speech recognition performance with limited amount of Nepali data by transferring knowledge
from large multilingual pre-training (Srivastava et al., 2018, Williams et al., 2023). Rijal et al.
(2024) fine-tuned Whisper on Nepali using available public datasets and found significant Word
Error Rate (WER) improvements. Nepali ASR is an emerging field of study with the availability
of open-source Nepali speech datasets on Hugging Face, such as community-contributed
corpora by pujanpaudel (2024), amitpant7 (2024), shunyalabs (2024), and maharjanabeeral
(2024).
In the comprehensive survey of Nepali ASR by Ghimire et al. (2024, 2023), it is noted that as
the fine-tuning data gradually becomes larger and larger for Nepali tasks, transformer based
encoder architectures do not perform as well as previously. This indicates that for tasks that
are primarily targeted towards multilingual coverage, there is an upper limit on the amount of
fine-tuning that can be done. The Conformer architecture (Gulati et al., 2020) overcomes this
ceiling. Conformer models outperform existing speech recognition models on conversational and
informal speech by leveraging Multi-Head Self-Attention to capture long-range acoustic context
and convolutional layers to model local acoustic patterns like phoneme boundaries. NVIDIA’s
NeMo framework (NVIDIA, 2023) offers to the production community two stage fine-tuning
modes: Training the decoder to the target language with the encoder frozen (Stage 1), and
unfreezing the whole model for a deeper fine-tuning (Stage 2). This incremental process is
especially appropriate in low resource areas where fine-tuning from a random initialisation might
lead to over-fitting.

--- PAGE 20 ---
2.5 Acoustic Challenges of Elderly Speech
One of the main challenges for this project is the acoustic difference between the training data
and target users. The findings by Vipperla et al. (2010) indicate that elderly speakers have a
higher ASR WER than younger adults because of breathiness, tremor and slower articulation
that is a result of physiological changes associated with aging (Presbyphonia). There is no public
elderly Nepali speech corpus, so that ASR model trained in this project is based on general
Nepali data. It is recognised that this mismatch is a limitation and ’formal elderly user evaluation’
validates this.
2.6 Intent Classification and Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning
Task-oriented dialogue systems rely heavily on the ability to detect the intent. Liu and Lane (2016)
demonstrated that attention-based recurrent networks perform better than simpler classifiers
for the joint intent and slot detection task. Chen et al. (2019) showed that fine-tuning BERT
significantly boosts the accuracy of intent detection on various datasets. In the context of
task-oriented voice assistants for daily living situations, intent classification needs to accurately
classify a diverse set of naturally-phrased queries, including queries of special concern, such as
‘emergency help’ where misclassification has real-world implications.
Hu et al. (2022) proposed the concept of LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) which adds low rank
matrices to the attention scores of a pre-trained model while keeping the pre-trained parameters
unchanged, making the number of trainable parameters fall to around 99%. This enables
fine-tuning a billion parameter model to be done on a single consumer GPU. There is not much
accuracy loss like Hu et al. report in several datasets when compared to full fine-tuning.
IndicBERT-v3 is a 1 billion parameter model pretrained on hundreds of billion Indic language
tokens comprising Nepali tokens released by the AI4Bharat group (Doddapaneni et al., 2023). It
is significantly easier to adapt for Nepali intent classification due to its pre training on Indic script
and grammar. The results show that the multiple intent classification approaches evaluated
showed plateau at around 80% accuracy on the 21 intent classification on MuRIL (Khanuja et al.,
2021), while DeBERTa (He et al., 2021) achieved 97.6% on 5 coarse intents classification but
does not scale with increase in model size, indicating that architecture and language-specific
pre training plays a more important role than model size alone.
5
--- PAGE 21 ---
2.7 Retrieval-Augmented Generation
Lewis et al. (2020) introduced RAG, a method that merges dense retrieval with autoregressive
generation to provide factual grounding for the output of LLMs—mitigating hallucination on
factual queries, but also addressing open-ended queries which do not have an explicit answer
in a single document. Reimers and Gurevych (2019) proved that the bi-encoder sentence
transformer generated embeddings that enabled efficient semantic retrieval, while Nogueira
and Cho (2019) found cross-encoder reranking to be a very effective approach to boosting the
precision by scoring the entire query-document pair.
2.8 Fall Detection
Casilari et al. (2017) reviewed wearable fall detection techniques, and concluded that threshold-
based methods on the resultant vector magnitude of the accelerometer are relatively simple to
compute and have acceptable sensitivity/specificity. Acceleration peaks during normal walking
are 12–20 m/s2, and during a fall are 30–60 m/s2
. Hijaz et al. (2010) combined fall detection and
automatic emergency calling, and demonstrated that automatic response effectively reduces
the delay between fall event and the first emergency call.
2.9 Critical Analysis and Gap Identification
The research gap that has emerged from existing work consists of three limitations. One of
the reasons for this is because almost all voice assistant research has been conducted in
high-resource languages, and there is no working and deployable voice assistant for Nepali
language (Shahi and Sitaula, 2021). Second, there is no validation of any Nepali ASR system for
elderly speakers, and there is no attempt to deal with the acoustic mismatch between the training
data and the elderly target users for Nepali. Third, none of the components that are needed for
elderly daily living in Nepal, such as medication reminders with voice confirmation, hands-free
emergency calling, fall detection, Bikram Sambat calendar support, elderly-paced TTS, and
local privacy-preserving operation are integrated in a single deployable application.

3 Project Aims, Objectives, and Scope
3.1 Project Aim
This is a project that aims to design, develop and test a voice assistant specifically for the needs
of senior citizens in the use of the Nepali language for an active, independent life.
3.2 Project Objectives
1. Fine tuning a Conformer-RNNT model (NVIDIANeMo) on a custom built and open source
Nepali speech database to build a voice user interface in the Nepali language for the
classification of 22 classes of intents associated with everyday tasks of the elderly.
2. To remind the user about his/her activities, such as taking medicine at a specific time, to
speak it out slowly and clearly in Nepali using the open source Sherpa-ONNX VITS TTS
engine and to verify with a voice when he/she responds.
3. To enable a 100% hands free service to make emergency calls and to dial the number of the
family member, the application will use a countdown and will automatically dial the number
of the family member when the user gives an emergency call.
4. To develop the functionalities into the system such as Date Checking, Bikram Sambat Cal-
endar and Festival Information, Weather and Music Playback using microservices backend
& Native Android APIs.
5. To test the entire system with a minimum of 5 real users (60+ years) in Nepal. Task Success
Rate, Intent classification accuracy, and system latency will be used for this evaluation.
When implemented, the technologies and extent of scope pertaining to each objective can be
more specific through iterative prototyping.
4 Project Scope
The project is conceived as a research prototype for an unserved user group: elderly people, who
are independent, Nepali speaking and have limited or no access to the internet in Nepal and only
communicate vocally in Nepali. The scope is intentionally limited, in this way to give this context
as much depth as possible, rather than a breadth in terms of the number of use-cases.
8
--- PAGE 24 ---
In scope: a fully functional Android App which can understand natural spoken Nepali commands
in 22 intents, voice controlled medication reminder in dual mode with confirmation, hands-
free emergency calling, falls detection by using an accelerometer, Bikram Sambat calendar,
time, weather, health support, and a formal evaluation with elderly persons in Nepal. The
system does not have to depend on data transmission from external sources, but rather on a
local WiFi network, intentionally so, because it is not a limitation of the resources available, in
accordance with the privacy and reliability requirements of the context in which the system is
being used.
Out of scope by principled decisions: iOS deployment (Android is more than 82% of the
smartphone market in Nepal (StatCounter Global Stats, 2024) and would be a ineffective to
develop an app for iOS); commercial-grade scalability (this is a research contribution and not
expected to be the release of a product); IoT integration and medical-monitoring certification
(outside of the project schedule and commercialization requirements for an undergraduate
prototype). For a pilot feasibility test, it is appropriate for the number of participants involved in
the formal evaluation to be larger than the stated minimum, which is not the case for large-scale
user evaluations exceeding the stated minimum. The heuristic that Nielsen (1994) states that
the required number of users to find usability problems is 5-7 is only applicable for recognizing
usability issues, and is not a general rule to validate the success of a task or the safety of a
feature. The evaluation is then conducted in a pilot study, which is sufficient to uncover major
usability issues and to estimate system-level TSR, but not be used to prove the safety of the
system, which would require a much larger and more demographically diverse population for a
proper validation.

Overall Approach
This project is using an Iterative Prototyping Methodology. The main deliverables are a running
software system, namely Nepali voice assistant designed for elderly Android users which
can be deployed locally. The major components (ASR, intent classifier, response generation,
TTS, mobile application) will be constructed as separate prototypes, tested quantitatively and

There are two types of data for the project. For fine tuning the ASR models in Nepali language

Methods by Objective
• ASR and Voice Interface (Objective 1): Pre-trained multilingual speech model will be

on multiple architectures with WER and CER. IndicBERT-v3-1b model will be used for intent

• Medication Reminders (Objective 2): There are two modes: normal (TTS alert with stop

• Emergency Calling (Objective 3): Voice activation SOS with dual emergency path and fall

• Date, time, and music (Objective 4): The data for time/date comes from the backend

• User Evaluation (Objective 5): A formal study of 5+ users aged 60+ in Nepal for 5 represen-

used will be Task Success Rate, with structured Nepali interviews focusing on voice clarity,
naturalness and ease of use. The ethics procedures of the BCU applies: verbal consent,
anonymised data, there are no real emergency numbers and the sessions are less than 30
minutes.
5.2 Justification of Methods
• Iterative Prototyping instead of Waterfall: Waterfall has all the decisions made beforehand,
which is incompatible with the ML development, where the accuracy cannot be predicted
before training. Agile Scrum was thought to be but discarded as it was designed for team
development; continuous iterations and periodic checks with the supervisor were better
suited for this solo job.
• Transfer learning over training from scratch: This project is not capable of training from
scratch since it would need millions of examples and months of compute. For low-resource
NLP, the competitive Nepali performance of the fine-tuned multilingual pre trained models
(NeMo Conformer, IndicBERT-v3) comes at a much lower cost.
11
--- PAGE 27 ---
• LoRA over Full Fine-Tuning: IndicBERT-v3-1B requires 40+ GB VRAM to be fully fine-
tuned, whereas the project has 8 GB VRAM. Given LoRa’s low number of parameters, it
is possible to fine-tune the model on consumer hardware. It is chosen over adapter and
prefix tuning for its better intent classification test results and established HuggingFace PEFT
tooling.
• Hybrid NLG over Pure LLM/Rule Based: Responses based on rules work well but fail
to produce the natural Nepali required for sensitive situations. LLM responses are slow
and costly, and hallucinate for factual queries. A hybrid: Python handlers for deterministic
queries, LLM persona prompting for open-ended responses, and RAG for out-of-vocabulary
queries is accurate, warm and has high coverage.
• Microservices instead of Monolithic Backend: Running separate FastAPI services in
isolated virtual environments will enable updating each service with minimal or no risk of
disrupting other services, and will provide the option to scale or replace each service without
affecting the others (Fowler and Lewis, 2014, Newman, 2015).
• Flutter over Native Android or React Native: Native Android is deep integration with high
costs of development, and no iOS path. React Native was turned down due to dynamic
typing dangers and native audio capabilities are not as robust as its native counterparts. In
Flutter, all the features are available as mature packages, and there is a single codebase in
Flutter (Google LLC, 2018).
• Local Network over Cloud APIs: Cloud services provide higher accuracy but are rejected
on two points: privacy: transmitting elderly users’ voice data to foreign servers without
meaningful informed consent is impractical; reliability: inconsistent connectivity in rural Nepal
is not suitable to a safety critical application.
• Formal Elderly User Study: Testing with young or adult is not an alternative to the actual de-
mographic. There are systematic differences between elderly speech and general-population
speech data, and safety-critical features need to be validated in the real world. Thus, the
objective of the project is to conduct tests with 5+ participants aged ≥ 60 years in Nepal.

6.2 Artefact Structure, Architecture, and Framework
6.2.1 Software Engineering and HCI Design Methodology
The artefact is a product of two methodological frameworks. From the software engineering point
of view, a Component Based Architecture (CBA) isolates each concern (speech recognition,
intent classification, response generation, speech synthesis) as a separate deployable unit with
well-defined interface. This directly supported the project’s most important architectural turn,
where Whisper was swapped with Conformer without changing anything in the Flutter client or
TTS service.
The interface at the Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) level is based on accepted principles of
elderly-friendly design. The home screen follows Fitts’s Law (Fitts, 1954, Stigall et al., 2019,
Siek et al., 2005) by restricting the number of buttons on the home screen to just two: a large
microphone button and an SOS button. Contrast standards are used throughout following
WCAG AAA requirements with a colour scheme of orange on dark (World Wide Web Consortium,
2023).This concrete accessibility parameter is summarized in Table 6.3.

The ASR service is based on a two-stage fine-tuned NeMo Conformer-RNNT model which is
chosen over Whisper for better conversational speech performance and parallelised low-latency
inference. Two-stage fine-tuning — Stage 1: Encoder is frozen, and Stage 2: Full Encoder

6.2.5 TTS Service Design
The TTS service applies custom acoustic wrapping to Sherpa-ONNX (k2-fsa, 2024) using the
Chitwan-NP VITS model (Kim et al., 2021) with an additional acoustic wrapper for elderly-specific
pacing, Devanagari number normalisation and engineering pauses at sentence boundaries.
Interrogative sentences are used with pitch modulation. The length_scale of 1.55 generates an
output in the range of 120-140 words per minute (wpm), in line with the recommendations of
speech-rate for elderly listeners with poor auditory processing speed (Pichora-Fuller and Souza,
2003).

6.2.6 Emergency Calling and SOS System Design
Two independent emergency pathways are found because under stressful conditions, elderly
users in a medical emergency might not be able to communicate and articulate voice com-
mands.
Pathway 1 — Voice-Triggered SOS: API will return an sos_countdown action, when the
classifier routes a query to sos_emergency or start_call. FlutterPhoneDirectCaller process the
countdown perfectly and displays a full screen countdown overlay, which, if not canceled in 10
seconds, automatically dials the emergency contact number, without any hands.
Figure 6.8 Emergency calling system: voice-to-sos flow diagram
Pathway 2 — Fall Detection Auto-Trigger: magnitude of the accelerometer R, which is dynamically calculated as:
The FallDetectionService reads the resultant
R= x2 + y2 + z2
If R is > 35.0 m/s2, a sequenced wake and call pipeline is fired: WakelockPlus keeps the CPU
from sleeping, a full screen intent notification wakes the display, a count down is triggered in
the UI isolate by an IPC event, on expiry, dual calls are fired from Activity context via Flutter-
PhoneDirectCaller and a fallback AndroidIntent(CALL) via the background service to ensure
the call completes even when the UI thread is dead. The threshold of 35.0 m/s2 is below the
reported fall floor of 38 m/s2 (Casilari et al., 2017, Hijaz et al., 2010) to allow for a safety margin
for real world falls which could have lower peak acceleration than the laboratory drop tests (e.g.
falls onto soft surfaces, partial catches).

6.2.7 RAG Fallback Design
The RAG fallback is used for queries not in the 22 classes trained or when the classifiers
are not very confident in their answers (typically less than 0.30), and follows a three-stage
process:
• query segmentation for Nepali conjunctions for independent sub-topic retrieval;
• dense retrieval using Qwen3-Embedding 0.6B and Qdrant (Qdrant, 2024) with top-20 candi-
dates retrieved using bilingual composite embedding strings (NEPALI: question | ENGLISH:
english_q | INTENT: intent) — a novel approach that benefits from English pre-training and
maintains the Nepali semantic content of the embedding;
• Reranking, filtering below 0.30 and top-3 injection to the Qwen-3.5 LLM call with prompt
elderly for the personalization.

Response Generation
The nine NLG approaches that were tried prior to the hybrid architecture are recorded in the

case.
Instead, quantised GGUF inference was employed with the Qwen-3.5 9B model (Qwen Team,
Alibaba Cloud, 2025) offloading all 40 layers of the model to the RTX 4060 GPU with Q4_K_XL
quantisation using llama.cpp, which achieved 26% reduction in latency from more than 30
seconds to less than 15 seconds without compromising the output quality. The Q4_K_XL variant
has been chosen for its ability to maintain higher precision in the weights of the attention layers,
resulting in more coherent Nepali output at a reasonable VRAM consumption of∼6 GB.

8 Critical Evaluation
8.1 Evaluation of the Artefact
Saathi is the first complete, fully offline, privacy preserving Nepali voice assistant, developed and
tested in the Nepal field. It directly tackles the lack of assistive technology for about 2.97 million
elderly Nepali speakers, empirical evidence of the fact that maximum performance of ASRs is
architectural rather than data-bound, and documents nine new failure modes of NLG for the
Nepali language, which have not been reported before, and is a template for replications for
other under-resourced Indic languages in the form of a microservices template.

8.1.4 Areas Falling Short and Honest Critique
Despite meeting all quantitative requirements, four areas require critical acknowledgement.
ASR-induced false emergency activations. All quantitative requirements are covered, but
four areas need to be critical acknowledged. Falses alarms due to ASR. Two health-related
inquiries were made to the SOS handler because a Devanagari transcription distortion of the
word टाउको दुख्दा (headache) created a phonemic similarity with the emergency words. The
error was generated at the ASR level, which means the classifier functioned properly on the
input – it is a confirmation gate downstream and NOT retraining the classifier. Both the affected
participants (U-04 and U-07) were visibly startled, as reported by Trajkova and Martin-Hammond
(2020) that one minor mistake may cause VA abandonment. The 10 second countdown can
be cancelled before the shot, making sure that the user doesn’t get an unexpected call; the
confirmation gate is a user-experience priority.
Missing usability metrics. Correctness measures are based on TSR and latency, not adoption.
There are no measures of this population as the session is too long due to time to learn, error
recovery rate and NASA-TLX cognitive load. If longitudinal sessions are planned for the future,
there should be a structured retryobservation and verbal cognitive load scale.
Conversational experience gap. No session memory, or 5.2-second mean latency, makes the
interaction command and control. All seven participants praised the speech’s clarity as well as
the capabilities of the hands-free operation, but none commented on the flow of conversation,
which is why we can confirm that the system is not used as a companion, but as a tool. This is
a design flaw, rather than a future feature.
RAG quality metric. The similarity of the keywords found in 6 prompts is a very basic indicator
of factual safety and cultural appropriateness and not medical safety. Future assessment should
be based on the assessment by LLM as judge and/or the structured rubric of Nepali cultural
appropriateness.
8.1.5 Critique of Methodology and Validity
The iterative prototyping process was successful with four of five pipeline elements redesigned
based on quantitative evidence, which was not possible under a waterfall approach.

Statistical interpretation. The headline result is statistically supported by a 95% Wilson interval
of 145/147 TSR [95.6%, 99.5]. The intent accuracy 95% CI is [86.7%, 94.2%] which is fairly
close to the lower bound of the CI, so the requirement is met, but there is some bandwidth
Threats to validity. The number of samples (n = 7) was not large enough for firm conclusions.
Selection of participants (urban residents of Kathmandu, presence of family members is an
advantage over the isolated elderly in rural Kathmandu as mentioned in the problem statement
and the results are generalised to the urban elderly with family members. However, Following
the guidelines of Nielsen (1994), n = 7 is enough for a pilot feasibility study to identify major
usability problems — and it did: the two false SOS alarms and the population query misrouting
were both revealed through this group. The 147 separate spoken interactions (21 scenarios × 7
participants) produce a Wilson 95% CI of [95.6%, 99. 5%], indicating that the TSR requirement
is achieved even under the worst-case assumptions; large-scale validation is an area for the
future work.
Reproducibility. The TSR and latency results are extracted from the PostgreSQL CONVERSATION_LOGS
with the script generate_charts.py on results/raw_results.csv which can be reproduced
by any researcher with the 147 recordings and model checkpoint. The FLEURS WER result
is reproducible without any doubt with the public ne_np split. The NLG evolution table (Sec-

repeating the failed attempts.

Research contributions:
1. 1st Real World Voice Assistant for Senior citizens in Nepali language. Combining
a culturally appropriate generation of NLG (natural language generation), the recognition
of ASR (automatic speech recognition), fall detection, and emergency response into a
deployable offline system tested with the target population, is something no existing system
has implemented. This is a system level contribution and not component level.
2. The need for a new ASR performance ceiling. Three Whisper trained systems stalled at
48–49% WER with no factor of training amount; a switch to Conformer achieved 10.10%
WER on the held-out set for this project and 30.50% for FLEURS, which suggests that the
ceiling is architectural, not data-bound.
3. Low-resource Nepali failure log. The hybrid architecture triumphed over 9 models that
failed beforehand, it is the gradient collapse of Gemma 3 1B that minimizes the duplication
of effort for researchers on similar languages (Google DeepMind, 2025).
Engineering contributions:
4. Bilingual composite RAG embedding strategy. Retrieval with Embedding “NEPALI: q |
ENGLISH: eq | INTENT: intent” is a practical problem in low-resource RAG: the embedding
model’s stronger pre-training on English is used, while preserving the Nepali semantics. This
strategy can be applied in cases where the strong embedding models are mostly English
pretrained, which is directly applicable to any language.
5. Two way, exact once reminder delivery in Android Doze. When used together with an in-
app polling of 5 seconds and a firedKeys deduplication map, an OS exactAllowWhileIdle
alarm and 100% delivery under aggressive OS power management can be achieved. Dual
channel alarm patterns are a well-known Android engineering technique but the combination
of grace window for health context, voice confirmation mode and deduplication is documented
here as a replicable implementation reference for mHealth reminder applications in resource
depleted environments.
6. Integration of Voice-SOS and accelerometer fall detection. Two fully independent
Emergency trigger paths with a single countdown, auto-dial and logging infrastructure,
both conscious and unconscious within a single Flutter app. The dual call mechanism
(FlutterPhoneDirectCaller primary; CALL intent fallback) guarantees that it is completed
even if the UI isolate is killed.

7. Schema for Interaction logging for aging population research. The PostgreSQL schema

8.3 Impact
8.3.1 Impact on the Target Community
With a population of 2.97 million people (10.21% of the national population) in 2021 (38.2%
higher than in 2011), Nepal has now started to become an ageing society by 2028 (Adhikari,
2023). In 2023, almost one million Nepalese emigrated from the country, creating a heavy
elderly population (Lamichhane, 2024). Older age groups are already largely excluded from
the use of digital health tools due to their English interfaces and literacy needs (Acharya et al.,
2023), and language barriers exacerbate exclusion for people living in rural areas (Acharya
et al., 2023). Saathi does not need any internet connection, any reading ability and is only in
Nepali.
The three functional areas relate to documented unmet needs. The technical reliability required
is implemented with the 100% delivery rate of Saathi and the medication reminders address
a critical gap, with up to 20% improvement in adherence in LMIC settings (Beratarrechea
et al., 2014). Fall detection and voice SOS decrease the time-to-first-contact for elderly living
alone (Hijaz et al., 2010). Bikram Sambat, tithi, and festival integration is a cognitive and cultural
need that Pal et al. (2019) have found to significantly enhance trust and adoption in the Global
South, and all seven UAT participants affirmed the alignment of this need. This prototype’s
approach to resolving the rural hardware barrier and its need for longitudinal rural validation and
caregiver training pathways are necessary for realising impact at scale, as addressed as future
work (Section 11).
8.3.2 Societal and Academic Impact
Saathi shows that it’s possible for a single researcher working on consumer hardware to build
a fully functioning, privacy-preserving voice assistant for a low-resource language. The three
microservice design is easily transferable to other under-resourced Indic language like Maithili,
Newari (Sharma et al., 2025), Limbu etc. There are three artefacts with direct academic reuse
value; the bilingual composite RAG embedding strategy (that can be used with any English
dominant embedding model in a low-resource language), the NLG failure log (rare negative

result documentation), and the 147 elderly Nepali audio recordings, the first known sample of
elderly Nepali speech, and foundation for a future dedicated corpus.
9 Conclusion
Around 2.97 million (Adhikari, 2023) elderly Nepalis speakers do not have an assistant that
understands their language, supports their daily lives and works offline. Youth migration has
reduced family support (Lamichhane, 2024) and there is no existing system available that has the
following features, which can all be deployed using a single application: Nepali ASR, knowledge
of cultural calendar, medication reminders, emergency response and fall detection. Saathi was
created to address this need.
Three empirical findings emerged from the process of iterative prototyping. First, the performance
of Nepali ASR is architectural: The WER was lowered from 48.56% to 10.10% when switching
from Whisper to Conformer on the held-out set and 30.50% on FLEURS, with no improvement
achieved when adding training data to Whisper. Second, reliable NLG for a low-resource
language must be based on the hybrid architecture: no single one of the nine individual LLM
models worked; all of the deterministic handlers, LLM persona prompting, and bilingual RAG
models succeeded where they failed alone. Third, elderly ASR is a routing problem, as the
elderly WER was 25.1%, but still, 98.6% Task Success Rate (95% CI: 95.6%–99.5%) was
achieved through the use of structural fallback.
All 5 project objectives achieved. Saathi v5.0 achieves 100% accuracy in transcribing Nepali
with 10.10% WER, 99.79% accuracy in validating those intents, was able to deliver reminders
100% of the time when the network was fully offline, was able to detect a fall with 100% accuracy
with no false positive, and completed all 147 queries with less than 15 seconds latency.
The wider impact is architectural: A full, private voice assistant for a poorly resourced language,
developed by one researcher on consumer devices. The three microservices design, bilingual
embedding of RAG, and a dual channel reliability model are transferable to other Indic languages.
Besides showing the working ones, the project also precisely locates the failures nine failure
modes in NLG, three dead ends inASR, and acoustic mismatch in the elderly that is safety-critical
misrouting.

A Feasibility, Risks, and Ethical Aspects
A.1 Revised Feasibility
Technical Feasibility The technical aspects of the project were fully open source, so it was
technically possible. Currently there are several major libraries that are also actively developed
and open source: NeMo, HuggingFace Transformers, PEFT, llama-cpp-python, Qdrant, Sherpa-
ONNX, Flutter, FastAPI and SQLAlchemy. The training was done on an NVIDIA RTX 4060
(8 GB VRAM) with the exception of Google Colab being used for Conformer Stage 2. The
microservices architecture resolved the CUDA dependency issues, while the hybrid GGUF
inference reduced VRAM usage from 40+ GB to 7–8 GB of peak VRAM.
Economic Feasibility Economic viability also was good. All the frameworks, models and
datasets utilized in this project, such as Hugging Face speech corpora, NeMo checkpoints,
IndicBERT-v3-1B, Qwen3 9B GGUF, Qwen3-Embedding, MiniLM-L12-v2, and Sherpa-ONNX
VITS are open source or available for free and non-commercial research use. Of course no
money had to be paid for services, proprietary APIs or commercial licence was required, and so
the price of the service was £ 0.
Operational Feasibility It is a consumer Android based system controlled by a standard
home Wi-Fi network, running on Android 9+ consumer hardware. With Android dominating
the smartphone market in Nepal, with the share of Android being 82% as per (StatCounter
Global Stats, 2024), this option ensures maximum accessibility. The mature users do not need
to go through the one-time user onboarding process, and the caregiver admin panel can be
programmed 100% without coding.
Time Feasibility The project was completed on time and all five project goals were accom-
plished within the project’s 32-week timeframe. These four significant architectural adaptations –
Whisper to Conformer, MuRIL to IndicBERT, Monolith to microservices, generative fine-tuning to
a hybrid NLG paradigm – were rolled out with iterative prototyping without any timeline stretches.
As scheduled, the formal user evaluation (Objective 5) was carried out in Weeks 25-27.

A.2 Updated Risk Analysis and Mitigation
In the course of the project, there were several events that were identified and actively resolved,
involving medium to high risks.
ASR Accuracy on Elderly Speech The most pronounced technical challenge was the poor
ASR performance on elderly speech which was addressed by a two-stage Conformer fine-tuning
approach to achieve a WER of 10.10% on all seven elderly speakers. If there are any routing
errors left after classification, the structural hybrid fallback layer absorbs them.
LoRA Gradient Collapse The potential impact of LoRA gradient collapse was high, although
it was a low probability risk. The loss was computed at each step of the learning and best
checkpoint was stored at each training step to ensure the loss of validation. It was required:
the Gemma 3 1B model collapsed at loss: 0.0000, gradient norm: NaN and was abandoned in
favor of IndicBERT LoRA model which was stable while training on both the runs.
Android Doze Mode Deferral The Doze Mode deferral of scheduled reminders risk was
deemed as a medium likelihood and high impact risk. This was solved by using 5 second polling,
exactAllowWhileIdle alarm and a grace period of 120 seconds, where all of the 40 test reminders
were successfully delivered on time, deferrals up to 88 seconds are transparently absorbed by
the alarm.
Inappropriate or Off-topic LLM Generation The temperature was lowered to 0.2 to ensure
that the LLM’s outputs would not be overly creative and therefore likely to be inappropriate or
off-topic, all LLM calls were capped at a maximum of 40-100 tokens, and an elderly-appropriate
persona prompt was added to all LLM calls to ensure the content kept on topic. If the question is
of a deterministic fact, it is directly sent to the Python handlers without involving the LLM.
Fall Detection False Positives The countdown time for fall detection was set to 10s which
is tunable, and the threshold of acceleration was set at default as 35.0 m/s2 to minimize false
positive fall detection, no fall detection was inappropriately triggered during 30 min of normal
use test.
ML Dependency Conflicts Multiple stacks for NeMo, HuggingFace and LLM inference resulted
in ML dependency conflicts being identified as high likelihood. All conflicts were solved by having
three completely separated Conda environments, with 3 different FastAPI microservices.
95
--- PAGE 111 ---
Elderly Participant Recruitment Elderly participants were recruited from a personal network
in Kathmandu, and seven 60-74 year old participants were recruited, sessions being held in
their own home and taking less than 30 minutes.
Emergency Intent Misclassification Finally, the most important risk (emergency intent mis-
classification) was mitigated two times with independent mitigations: the first one being a special
dedicated button for SOS on the screen which bypasses the classifier altogether and the second
one being the accelerometer-based fall detection which starts the SOS pathway without voice
inputs. The model was also trained with the usage of class-weighted LoRA loss to ensure that
the sos_emerggency intent is prioritized among other intent classes.
A.3 Revised Ethical Considerations
Ethical protection was put into place throughout the project.
Informed Consent Before the evaluation session, a simple Nepali verbal explanation was
provided, and a Nepali information sheet was handed out, and informed consent was noted using
simple written signatures or a witnessed verbal consent (for those who had not good written
literacy). Withdrawal at any time was not to have any effect and was specifically stated.
Voice Data Anonymisation Data related to voices was anonymised at collection. Each
recording was labeled with P03_S12_identity_connections [date.wav] and the label to identify
connections were recorded in a password protected document on BCU OneDrive. All recordings
were deleted from the personal device within 24 hours after they were uploaded and no voice
data was ever transferred to an external server or third party system throughout the project.
Safe SOS Testing Safety testing for SOS/fall detection was performed without harming the
subjects. For testing SOS auto-dial it was only tested with a non-operational number, and the
CALL_TEST_MODE was set true for the whole test, no real emergency numbers were dialled,
and participants were informed of this before each test was conducted.
Safe Fall Detection Testing The fall detection threshold calibration was conducted using a
fall detector in a padded case dropped from a fixed height onto a padded mat; no humans were
involved.

Participant Wellbeing Wellbeing of participants was taken into account throughout the evalua-
tion process. Tasks were delivered and prompts were given in Nepali and post session interviews
were also held in Nepali. Sessions were no longer than 30 minutes and a caregiver/family
member was offered the option to be present, but only 5 of seven accepted.
Data Minimisation The use of data minimisation was done satisfactorily. There was no
retention of interaction logs – only audio recording and anonymised task outcome logs and data
was extracted after. This report displays only aggregated and anonymised data, such as task
success rates, WER/CER scores and latency. The project was a research prototype, and no
data of any participant was used in any commercial way or for third parties’ profiling.

or IRB approval was required. Throughout, BCU Research Ethics Policy was followed.

I.3 Participant Information and Consent Procedure
The following was orally communicated to the researcher (in Nepali) prior to each session:
1. Purpose. To try out a voice assistant developed in Nepali language for elderly people. To
sum up: The system is not commercial but a research prototype.
2. Data collected. Limit questions to voice (only questions and pass/fail task results recorded).
The information collected did not include personal health information, aside from the infor-
mation participants provided during the session.
3. Data storage. Recordings anonymised right away using alphanumeric codes (e.g. P03_S12).
Using a password protected mapping file on BCU OneDrive. Recordings made on personal
devices and deleted within 24 hours of upload.
4. Voluntary participation. No penalties for dropping out and it was voluntary.
5. Safe testing. In SOS testing (CALL_TEST_MODE=true flag is turned on throughout the test),
no real emergency calls would be created. There is no human detection of falls while
parameters of thresholds are being calibrated. Verbal consent was witnessed with the family
member, taking into consideration the different levels of literacy. A total of 5 of 7 were with a
