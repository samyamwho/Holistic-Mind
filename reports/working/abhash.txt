Abstract
Independent travellers planning a trip to Nepal today must research, filter, and reconcile informa-
tion scattered across static informational sites, tour-operator pages, and informal blogs, with the
practical localised information they need often sparse or out of date. This project addresses the
planning problem through AI agents driven travel concierge that turns a free-form chat message
into a personalised day-by-day Kathmandu Valley itinerary, guiding user through place selection
from semantic retrieval based place recommendations, hotel slection from DBSCAN based
spatial cluster group hotel recommendations, and finally itinerary generation. The retrieval
foundation is all-MiniLM-L6-v2, a small bi-encoder fine-tuned on 5,500 synthetic query-to-fact
pairs generated with the help of large language models, scored against a knowledge base in
which every place is decomposed into atomic single-claim facts, with each place ranked by
averaging the similarity of its top three matching facts. The artefact is orchestrated as four
phase-isolated AI agents with backend tools that return short acknowledgements, so the panel
state the user sees comes from the backend rather than from AI agent text. Evaluation against
three off-the-shelf retrieval baselines, latency profiling over thirty controlled trips, and a small
user acceptance test show that Recall@5 climbs from 0.77 on the off-the-shelf MiniLM baseline
to 0.94 on the fine-tuned retriever, a 15.6% lift over the strongest sparse baseline, with the
retrieval pipeline execution holding a median of 87.9 ms and the day-by-day itinerary with a
14.8 s median and 18.1 s at 90th percentile.
Keywords: Nepal tourism, AI travel concierge, multi-agent AI, semantic retrieval, fine-tuned
bi-encoder, knowledge base, synthetic query generation

1.0 Introduction
Tourism is a huge industry in Nepal contributing to about 7% to GDP of Nepal with around
16% employable generation working in the industry (Awale, 2025). The usage of internet
has shifted the way travelers gather their information. In the context of Nepal, about 94% of
tourists visiting Nepal utilize ICT, with 62% learning about Nepal from the internet (Kharel, 2024).
Leveraging ICT tools and technologies is a promising approach to boost the tourism industry in
Nepal. Among the modern technologies, AI powered travel planners are recognized as the next
transformative change in the travel and tourism industry which can bring comfort, efficiency, and
personalization to travelers (Honmane, 2025). This is supported by Digital Nepal Framework 2.0
which recommends AI initiatives to boost tourist satisfaction in Nepal (Ministry of Communication
and Information Technology, 2025b).
1.1 Problem Statement
Travel related information in Nepal is fragmented (Shrestha et al., 2024) across static informa-
tional websites or tour operator websites. As tourists often prefer independent travel without
having their tours managed (Dahal et al., 2024), substantial time is spent on manually research-
ing, analyzing and filtering travel information which has created a crucial need for personalized
tourism recommendations for travellers (Honmane, 2025; Shrestha et al., 2021).

2.1 Addressing the Cold Start Problem
A consistent challenge highlighted across the surveyed literature is the “cold start” problem.
Shrestha et al. (2024) highlight that reliance on interaction data is futile in low-resource regions
such as Nepal and instead propose machine learning models trained on survey data that generate
recommendations solely from static user attributes. Islek and Oguducu (2020) propose the
use of fixed-dimension vector embeddings generated from text descriptions of places, allowing
recommendations immediately without prior interaction history. While Shrestha et al. (2024)
achieve high accuracy, their reliance on static and labour-intensive survey data raises scalability
questions compared to the automated semantic extraction methods used by Islek and Oguducu
(2020). A more scalable solution is proposed by Dahal et al. (2024), where a content-based
recommendation is formulated by calculating cosine distance between a genre-bin vector of
places and a user preference vector derived from preference data collected during initial login.
Similarly, a more advanced solution to the cold start problem is implemented by Noorian et al.
(2023), where instead of relying on interaction history an asymmetric similarity schema derived
from social media posts is utilised.

2.2 Evolution from Static Filtering
The literature shows that the domain of TRS has evolved noticeably from static filtering techniques
towards hybrid architectures. Heavy reliance on a single algorithm often failed to capture the
multifaceted nature of tourist decision making. To address this, M and C (2021) proposes a
weighted hybridisation of recommendations from User-Based CF, Item-Based CF, a popularity-
based model, and ArcGIS, suggesting that the disadvantages of individual algorithms could
be overcome by another. Similarly, Dahal et al. (2024) proposes a hybrid approach combining
Collaborative Filtering (CF) and Content-Based Filtering (CBF) based on cosine similarities
of weighted user and place vectors, where the intersection of the two sets is surfaced as
top recommendations. While M and C (2021) focused on Jaipur city, Dahal et al. (2024)
contextualised the approach for Nepal by collecting a dataset from online sources and highlighting
the necessity of such a system in regions where digital footprints are fragmented. Shrestha et al.
(2024) further supports this stance, demonstrating through survey-based demographic profiling
of 2,400 international and domestic tourists in Pokhara that refinement of place recommendations
from trained machine learning algorithms using a novel parametric weighted algorithm produces
better recommendations than systems such as Google Maps.
2.3 Shift towards Semantic Based and Sequential Recommendations
Despite the success of hybrid architectures, they still fail to account for the semantic and
sequential nature of travel. Recognising every trip as a narrative rather than a set of isolated
choices, the literature shows a shift towards deep learning approaches. Islek and Oguducu
(2020) advances semantic processing by fine-tuning BERT models to transform text descriptions
into dense vectors. Huang (2024) critiques traditional CBF, CF, and CNN algorithms for their
inability to identify long-range dependencies effectively, proposing the BERT4Rec model for a
more profound understanding of sequential user behaviour. Building on this sequential paradigm,
Noorian et al. (2023) introduces a BERT model for identifying sequential Points of Interest (POI)
from raw Top-N recommended POIs. Instead of relying solely on rating numbers, the authors
implement LSTM for feature extraction by transforming unstructured review texts into structured
fixed-dimension semantic vectors used to compute more accurate similarity scores between
users, strengthening the collaborative filtering approach to capture the multifaceted nature of
opinions expressed in reviews.
2.4 Context-Aware and Environmental Integration
Static recommendation approaches are increasingly viewed as obsolete as the integration of
context such as geolocation and environmental factors is emerging as a critical determinant of
tourism recommendation accuracy. Noorian et al. (2023) demonstrated that integrating weather,
temperature, and visit-time contexts significantly enhances the relevance of recommendations
compared to non-contextual approaches. This aligns with the work of M and C (2021), who

integrated the ArcGIS API for spatial context and argued that proximity is a weighted factor of
equal importance to user preferences. However, Shrestha et al. (2024) expands the definition of
‘context’ beyond the physical, incorporating planning attributes, budget constraints, preference
signals, and satisfaction signals. A clear theoretical split is observed in which some researchers
treat context as environmental or physical while others view it as situational and socio-economic.
2.5 Critical Analysis and Gap Identification
The existing literature reveals that hybrid frameworks yield better recommendations than stan-
dalone methods. However, hybrid frameworks rely on linear combinations (M and C, 2021) and,
when not integrated with deep learning layers, fail to capture semantic nuances. Although deep
learning architectures are capable of capturing semantic nuances and sequential dependencies
(Huang, 2024; Islek and Oguducu, 2020; Noorian et al., 2023), they introduce high computational
overhead to train and deploy, especially in resource-limited environments. Furthermore, the
solutions in Nepal proposed by Shrestha et al. (2024) and M and C (2021) rely on static datasets
and fail to account for the dynamic nature of travel such as environmental changes. This creates
an apparent dilemma, since large transformer models typically require vast volumes of data to
avoid overfitting (Noorian et al., 2023), yet local data remains fragmented; recent work on small,
domain-fine-tuned sentence transformers suggests that a few thousand curated pairs can be
sufficient for a focused tourism domain, narrowing this gap. Consequently, there is a distinct
lack of research applying context-aware, semantic transformer-based recommendation to the
fragmented travel data available in Nepal, and no published work that combines such retrieval
with a multi-agent conversational planner.
2.6 Project Justification
Addressing the critical gap of localised solutions in Nepal failing to account for the dynamic
nature of travel, this project proposes an AI-agent-driven travel concierge that combines a
domain-fine-tuned semantic retriever with live external context tools at the itinerary stage. To
address the distinct lack of research applying semantic transformer-based recommendation
to fragmented travel data in Nepal, the project fine-tunes a small BERT-family bi-encoder
(sentence-transformers all-MiniLM-L6-v2) on synthetic query-to-fact pairs drawn from a hand-
curated Kathmandu Valley knowledge base, with each place decomposed into atomic single-
claim facts so the retriever matches on one facet of a place without being diluted by unrelated
content. To close the additional gap around multi-agent conversational planning in low-resource
tourism domains, the system is organised as four phase-isolated agents (place, hotel, itinerary,
narrator) on Google ADK, with environmental and live context (weather, festivals, permits, news)
integrated at the itinerary generation step rather than into the place ranking itself. By combining
a deep semantic retriever with phase-isolated agent orchestration and live context grounding,
the project addresses the cold-start problem inherent to the domain and delivers a responsive
tool capable of surfacing lesser-known places through semantic alignment and live context,

3.0 Project Aims, Objectives, and Scope

3.1 Project Aim
The aim of this project is to design, build, and evaluate AI agents driven travel concierge that
walks an independent traveller from a free-form chat message through personalised place and
hotel recommendations to a day-by-day Kathmandu Valley itinerary, grounded in a localised
knowledge base curated for Nepal.

4.3 Dataset Construction Methodology
The retrieval model was trained on a hand-curated set of 5,500 query-to-fact pairs. The knowl-
edge base behind those pairs is 156 places, each decomposed into single-claim atomic facts
for a total of 546 facts. Queries were written by hand in the keyword-bag style that the place
agent emits at run time, so the training distribution matches what the system actually receives
rather than the free-form questions a tourist might type. The split holds out facts rather than
queries: 10% of facts are reserved for validation, and any query linked to a held-out fact moves
with it. This rules out information leakage and forces the model to retrieve claims it has never
seen during training. At evaluation time the search corpus is the full set of facts, but only the
validation queries are issued against it. Section 4.4 specifies the metrics used.

5.2.3 System Architecture
The artefact runs as three cooperating local processes together with two external cloud services.
The three local processes refer to: a web interface, a backend server, and an agent server. Their
general layout is depicted in Figure 5.2. Only one architectural invariant determines the rest of
the design: the backend server being the only client of the database. The web interface never
accesses the database directly, and the agent server does not access it at all. This separation
means every panel rendered in the user interface is driven from backend state rather than from
an agent’s text reply, which prevents a class of hallucination bugs in which a recommendation
refers to a place or hotel the database does not contain. The agent server only contains the
orchestration logic. The language-model inference is performed using Google’s Gemini API,
and the itinerary agent also gets information about festivals, permits, and disruptions from Tavily.
In production, each component is running inside Docker.

First pattern is the kick-off. When the user confirms one phase, the backend will set up an

see. Three such flags are used, one per phase. After each chat turn the backend reads the
relevant flag, resets it, and forwards the boolean to the frontend, which uses the signal to decide
whether to refetch the panel.

5.2.4.3 Place Recommendation: Semantic Retrieval
Query expansion techniques in information retrieval justify converting user intent into a short
keyword-based query (Azad and Deepak, 2019). So, the place agent simply prepares and a
short keyword-bag query that is the essence of the user’s articulated interests and sends to
the backend which then forms a ranked list of places. The query goes through the fine-tuned
bi-encoder which outputs a 384-dimensional vector that is scanned and compared against the
vector representations of every Fact using cosine similarity. The backend retains just the best
three facts per place and averages their similarity scores to come up with one place score. This
top-K-average method rewards places whose best features are essentially a good match with
the query, without giving one high match result the power to overshadow the rest of the evidence.
If a user at a later point asks a factual question about a specific named place, the agent does
not follow the search path but rather accesses a separate lookup tool providing the curated facts
for that place which the agent can use to ground its response on.

5.2.4.4 Hotel Recommendation: Spatial Clustering
Once a user confirms places selection, the hotel agent is in charge of allocating lodging intelli-
gently. Suggesting hotels without considering the geographical location of selected places could
result in itineraries that are impractical. The backend first groups the places into regional bases
by using a DBSCAN-style proximity rule (Ester et al., 1996) with an ε of about 30 kilometres.
Each connected component corresponds to a cluster or base which is then ordered by size and
given labels. For every base, the backend gets a list of hotels located within about 25 kilometres
of the cluster centroid as the candidate pool. Then, the hotel agent makes filtering specification
describing four different axes: type of accommodation, miminum hotel price, minimum rating,
and required amenities. After that, predefined maximum number of hotels are selected per
base to ensure hotel selection for user remains managable. Geometry of bases are stored on a
session so that later refinement rounds reuse the same bases and only filter the hotels per base
as required. The whole process is shown in Figure 5.6. As with the place phase, any questions
about the facts of a particular named hotel are passed to a separate lookup tool which brings
the hotel catalog attributes, so the agent answers from the curated record rather than inventing
details.

6.2.3 System Performance Measurement
The data comes from 30 controlled trips on the development host, extracted via the queries
in Appendix C. The first assistant turn in each phase is the kickoff and carries that phase’s
main work: semantic place search in phase 1, hotel clustering and search in phase 2, full
day-by-day itinerary generation in phase 3. Later turns are refinements that revise earlier output
after a user follow-up. Three views of the data follow: retrieval alone, the full chat turn, and a
parallel-versus-sequential narrator comparison.
6.2.3.1 Retrieval Pipeline
The retrieval pipeline is essentially free (Table 6.17), with medians of 87.9 ms on kickoff and
92.3 ms on refinement and almost every call under 200 ms. The single 1638 ms outlier
corresponds to the first call after process start, when the embedding model loads into memory.
Once warm, retrieval contributes only a small fraction of the chat turn’s total time.

is to Nepal, that combination of grounded recommendations, faster turns and locally aware
planning is the more useful product.
7.2 Impact
The impact discussion below mirrors the three problems set out in Section 1.1, and the persistent
gap in up-to-date data, and closes with the wider methodological contribution the project offers
beyond its named beneficiary.
7.2.1 Reducing Information Fragmentation for Independent Travellers
The first problem set out in Section 1.1 is that travel information for Nepal is scattered across
static informational sites, tour-operator pages, and informal blogs, leaving the independent
traveller to research, filter, and reconcile facts by hand. The artefact collapses that workload into
a single chat. Every recommendation the user sees is tied to a curated knowledge-base entity
rather than a free-text generation, and the per-phase response targets in Section 6.2.3 keep the
conversation moving. The scope of this impact, however, is bounded by the Kathmandu Valley
and outward seed locations declared in Section 3.3.
7.2.2 Delivering Localised, Nepal-Specific Information
The second problem is the shortage of reliable, localised information on the practical aspects
of travelling in Nepal. The artefact answers this through the curated Nepal-specific catalogue
described in Section 6.1.1 and the retriever calibrated to that domain in Section 6.1.2, with
itineraries priced in NPR across the three nationality tiers so the answers a non-SAARC visitor
Page 76
--- PAGE 90 ---
BIRMINGHAM CITY UNIVERSITY
SCHOOL OF COMPUTING AND DIGITAL TECHNOLOGY
sees differ from those a Nepali user sees. The impact widens as the catalogue grows: the same
seed mechanism in Section 6.1.1 would absorb localised guidance on transport, accommodation,
costs, safety, health and hygiene, internet availability, and general travel logistics, each surfaced
through the same chat without changing the architecture. The audience is currently bounded by
the English-only language scope acknowledged in Section 8.3.
7.2.3 Addressing the Persistent Up-to-Date Information Gap
The third problem, raised under successive editions of the Digital Nepal Framework and revisited
in Section 1.1, is the persistent absence of up-to-date information for travellers. The artefact
narrows that gap on two fronts. The live external tools in Section 6.1.4.5 fetch festival, permit,
and weather signals at request time rather than reading them from a cached snapshot, and
the CSV-driven seed described in Section 6.1.1 makes every catalogue extension a one-row
change rather than a code release. Coverage is still limited to three external categories, leaving
wider transport and hyper-local sources for further enhancements.
7.2.4 Wider Impact
The project also leaves behind a small open resource for low-resource tourism research. The
5,500 query-to-fact pairs and the 156-place curated catalogue produced through Section 6.1.1
and Section 6.1.2 can be reused without paying the cold-start curation cost, and the curate-
then-fine-tune-then-serve pipeline running through Section 6.1.1 to Section 6.1.6 doubles as a
working template for adjacent low-resource regions.
The Digital Nepal Framework 1.0 (Ministry of Communication and Information Technology, 2019)
and the Digital Nepal Framework 2.0 (Ministry of Communication and Information Technology,
2025b) both identify tourism as a priority sector and the persistent staleness of localised travel
information as a problem to solve. The artefact offers an independent reference point for the
policy-level conversation those frameworks set out to drive, demonstrating concrete progress
on the gap they raise.
The most direct economic lift sits with Nepal’s small operators. Independent and family-run
places and hotels that rarely rank on global platforms appear in the catalogue in Section 6.1.1
on equal footing with larger establishments, lifting discoverability for businesses that would
otherwise stay invisible to first-time travellers, and the booking flow drafted in the artefact leaves
a clear extension point for local operators to plug into directly.
8.0 Conclusion
Nepal’s tourism economy leans on independent travellers who today plan their trips through a
scattered web of static sites, tour-operator pages, and informal blogs where localised, up-to-date
information is sparse. In response, the project delivers AI agents driven travel concierge that
turns a free-form chat message into a personalised day-by-day itinerary, scoped for now to the
Page 77
--- PAGE 91 ---
BIRMINGHAM CITY UNIVERSITY
SCHOOL OF COMPUTING AND DIGITAL TECHNOLOGY
Kathmandu Valley as the first region of coverage. The system is grounded in a hand-curated
knowledge base of 156 places decomposed into 546 atomic single-claim facts alongside a
catalogue of 100 hotels. A synthetic dataset of 5,500 query-to-fact pairs was then generated with
the help of large language models and used to fine-tune the sentence-transformers all-MiniLM-
L6-v2 bi-encoder, with each place ranked at query time by averaging the cosine similarity of
its top three matching facts so a single strong match cannot drown out broader evidence. The
planning workflow is split across phase-isolated AI agents: semantic place retrieval, DBSCAN
spatial clustering for hotels grouped around the confirmed places, and itinerary generation
with nationality-tiered NPR pricing for Nepali, SAARC, and non-SAARC travellers grounded
in live weather, festival, permit, and disruption context, with backend tools owning the panel
state so no hallucinated place or hotel ever reaches the user. The artefact cleared every target:
Recall@5 climbed from 0.77 on the off-the-shelf MiniLM baseline to 0.94 on the fine-tuned
retriever, the retrieval pipeline held an 87.9 ms median against the 500 ms target, the itinerary
kickoff cleared its 30-second budget at 14.8 s median and 18.1 s at the 90th percentile, every
functional and non-functional user requirement was validated end-to-end, and a ten-participant
user acceptance test returned a mean System Usability Scale score of 73.0 above the 68
industry benchmark. Beyond the numbers, the artefact narrows the three problems set out at
the start of this report for independent travellers visiting Nepal, lifts discoverability for the small
local operators who rarely rank on global platforms, and leaves behind a transferable template
that, with a rebuilt knowledge base and a re-run fine-tune, can serve any region whose tourism
information is similarly fragmented.

B Feasibility, Risks, and Ethical Aspects
This appendix revisits the three planning concerns originally set out at the proposal stage and
reports their state at the end of the project. Section B.1 explains why the chosen scope and stack
were achievable within the available time and resources, Section B.2 lists the risks that shaped

--- PAGE 101 ---

the project’s design decisions and the mitigations carried out against each, and Section B.3 sets
out the ethical considerations that match the final artefact.
B.1 Feasibility
The project was achievable within the available time, data, and resources because every major
architectural choice was scoped to fit a single-student, single-machine budget. Transfer learning
rather than training from scratch reduced the data requirement from millions of examples to
thousands, making a hand-curated dataset of 5,500 synthetic query-to-fact pairs adequate
for fine-tuning a small bi-encoder. Decoupling embedding generation from retrieval, with em-
beddings pre-computed offline and indexed for approximate-nearest-neighbour lookup, kept
real-time work down to query encoding and a vector similarity scan, both of which run comfortably
on the development host without specialised hardware.
Software dependencies were chosen to favour open-source and locally reproducible components:
PostgreSQL with pgvector for storage and retrieval, Django for the backend, Google ADK for the
agent runtime, and React for the frontend. The agent layer is phase-isolated, so each agent’s
prompt stays short enough for a single student to maintain and iterate without coordination
overhead.
Data feasibility rests on the 156-place hand-curated knowledge base, which is bounded enough
to be built by one person over the project timeline while still being large enough to exercise
every functional capability the artefact claims. Live external context is gathered through weather,
festival, and news APIs that all offer free developer tiers within the project’s usage volume.
Finally, the author’s prior experience in full-stack web development and AI-agent engineering
provided the skill base needed to integrate the three application layers, and the iterative sprint-
based delivery left each layer in a runnable state before the next began, so feasibility was tested
continuously rather than deferred to a single end-of-project integration.

B.3 Ethical Considerations
The original ethical concern centred on third-party review data and platform terms of service.
The final artefact does not use any review data, so that concern no longer applies and is replaced
with a set of considerations that match the system actually built.
Human participants in the user acceptance test (Section 6.2.6) gave informed consent through the
Google Form, including age confirmation, an explicit opt-in to anonymised use of their responses,
and the right to withdraw at any point. No personally identifying information was collected:
participants are referred to in the report only as P1 through P10, with raw responses retained in
Appendix D and the underlying dataset kept securely for twelve months after submission before
being deleted.
User account data in the system is intentionally minimal. Only an email, an optional display name
and nationality, and a SHA-256 password hash are stored; no payment data, no government
identifier, and no location data are collected at any point. Sessions can be planned anonymously
without registration, and the post-itinerary registration prompt is dismissible so users who do not
wish to create an account are not blocked from using the system.

The knowledge base was hand-curated from publicly available online sources on Kathmandu
Valley tourism, with attribution preserved against each source so the curation is auditable. The
synthetic query-to-fact pairs used to fine-tune the retriever were generated with the help of
large language models (Gemini and Claude, cited in Section 6.1.2); every generated pair was
reviewed by hand before training, and the use of LLMs in dataset construction is openly disclosed
rather than hidden.
External API calls send only the minimum information needed to perform a lookup: weather
queries carry a location and a date range, the news tool carries the phrasing of the live-context
query, and Gemini receives the agent prompt plus the user’s chat turn. No participant identifiers,
account details, or unrelated session content are forwarded to external services.
Socially, the system is positioned to lift discoverability for small local operators that rarely rank on
global platforms (Section 7.2.4) and to keep recommendations grounded in a curated knowledge
base rather than free-text generation, reducing the risk of misleading travellers about places
that do not exist. These design decisions form the ethical backbone of the project and have
been honoured throughout development and evaluation.
